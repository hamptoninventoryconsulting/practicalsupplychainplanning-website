A planner can have a sensible forecast, reliable lead times and well-chosen planning settings, yet still see shortages or excess stock appearing unexpectedly. Often the problem starts with a much simpler question: is the stock shown in the system really there?

Stock accuracy is not just a warehouse measure. It is the starting point for every planning decision. If that starting point is wrong, the plan projects the error forward.

The number in the system is the planner's starting point

Stock accuracy is how closely the on-hand quantity recorded in the system matches the quantity physically available. It needs to be assessed item by item and location by location.

A business may reconcile its total inventory value or total unit count and conclude that stock is accurate overall. That can be misleading. One item may be overstated by 100 units while another is understated by 100 units. The total may balance, but neither item can be planned correctly.

The same applies across locations. Stock held in one warehouse does not help a customer-facing branch, production line or distribution centre unless it can be moved there in time. A correct total across the network can still hide a shortage at the location where demand actually occurs.

For planning purposes, the useful question is not, "Does our inventory total look right?" It is:

Does this item, at this location, have the quantity the system says it has?

Why a small opening error affects the whole plan

Most planning processes are time-phased. They begin with today's on-hand stock, then project that stock forward as future demand consumes it and future supply replenishes it.

That means the current on-hand figure is not just a reportable balance. It is an input to every future planning calculation.

Imagine a planner believes there are 100 units available. The plan expects demand of 25 units each week, so it projects stock running out in week four and plans supply to arrive before then.

If physical stock is actually only 50 units, the item will run out around two weeks earlier than the plan suggests. The system may not plan supply soon enough because it believes another 50 units exist.

The opposite is equally problematic. If the system shows 50 units but there are really 100, the plan brings replenishment forward unnecessarily. The resulting order can create excess stock, use warehouse space and tie up cash that could have been used elsewhere.

The planning system is not necessarily making a poor decision. It is making a logical decision based on an incorrect starting figure.

Too much stock on paper causes late supply

When system stock is overstated, planners see inventory that is not physically available. Future demand appears covered for longer than it really is, so replenishment is delayed.

Eventually the physical stock runs out. At that point, the business may discover the discrepancy only when a picker cannot find the product, a production order cannot be issued, or a customer order cannot be fulfilled.

The immediate reaction is often to expedite supply. But expediting treats the symptom. The underlying issue was that the plan was allowed to rely on stock that did not exist.

This is particularly damaging for items with long replenishment times. Once the shortage is discovered, there may be little ability to recover quickly.

Too little stock on paper creates excess

Understated stock produces the reverse outcome. The system sees a shortage earlier than it should and recommends supply too soon or in a larger quantity than necessary.

That can look less urgent than a stockout, but it still has a cost. Extra inventory consumes working capital, takes up space and may eventually become slow-moving or obsolete. It can also distort decisions about supplier capacity, production loading and warehouse requirements.

A planner may then be challenged about why inventory is high, even though the original cause was an inaccurate on-hand balance.

How inaccuracies build up quietly

Stock errors do not usually come from one dramatic event. More often, they accumulate through routine activity.

A receipt may be entered incorrectly. A damaged item may be discarded without a write-off transaction. Production scrap may not be recorded. A backflush or BoM consumption transaction may not have calculated correctly. A BoM yield factor may be inaccurate. A picker may take more or less than the quantity confirmed in the system. An item may be transacted in cartons when someone is thinking in individual units. An item may have been physically moved but no system movement occurred. A PO receipt may have used the delivery document quantity without the warehouse checking that the stated quantity was accurate.

Timing also matters. Physical stock can move before the related transaction is recorded, or a transaction may be posted before the physical movement takes place. Short delays can be manageable if they are understood and consistent. Repeated or uncontrolled delays make it difficult to know whether the system balance represents reality at any given moment.

These issues are easy to dismiss individually. Over time, though, they can create enough difference to change the plan.

Count to learn, not just to adjust

Cycle counting is one of the most practical ways to measure and improve stock accuracy. Rather than shutting down to count everything once or twice a year, the warehouse counts a small group of items regularly.

Correcting the system quantity is necessary, but it is only the first step. The greater value comes from investigating why the difference occurred - root cause analysis.

If the same item repeatedly has count variances, simply adjusting it each time does not solve the problem. The business needs to understand the process behind the error. Is the item difficult to count? Is it stored in more than one place? Are units of measure unclear? Is damage being handled outside the normal transaction process? Are picks or receipts being confirmed inaccurately? I once found the reason for an annual stock take variance of hundreds of thousands of dollars that was due to a BoM yield factor. Fix the root cause, fix the stock accuracy, better reflect actual Cost of Goods sold.

A useful cycle-count process turns discrepancies into process improvements. Over time, that reduces both the need for adjustments and the number of planning decisions based on unreliable data.

Prioritise the items where accuracy changes a decision

Not every item carries the same planning risk.

Fast-moving items deserve close attention because a small quantity error can disappear quickly through normal demand. An error of 20 units may be insignificant for a slow-moving item that sells a few units a month, but it could represent a day's demand for another item.

Items where supply is needed soon also deserve attention. This is the point where a small difference can change the answer to an important question: do we need to order now, or can we wait?

For example, if the projection shows stock running out just after the next order would arrive, an unrecorded loss may mean the order should already have been placed. If the record is understated by the same amount, an unnecessary order may be planned.

This does not mean slow-moving items can be ignored. They may be high value, difficult to replace or essential for a customer or production process. But when deciding where to focus routine counting effort, consider where an accuracy error is most likely to alter a supply decision.

Inaccurate stock leads to broken promises

Stock accuracy affects more than replenishment. It also affects the delivery dates given to customers.

Available to promise (ATP) works out what can be committed to new customer orders by starting with on-hand stock, taking away what is already allocated to existing orders, and adding planned supply as it arrives. Like the replenishment plan, it starts from the on-hand figure.

If on-hand stock is overstated, ATP shows stock as available when it is not. A customer is promised delivery from stock that does not exist, and the business finds out only when the order cannot be picked. The order is then late, and the customer has to be told the original date will not be met.

If on-hand stock is understated, ATP shows less available than there really is. Customers may be given later dates than necessary, or orders may be turned away even though stock is sitting on the shelf.

Either way, the order-promising process can be working correctly and still give the wrong answer, because the starting figure is wrong. Reliable promises depend on accurate stock.

Stock errors are a form of supply variation

Safety stock is there to protect against variation: demand that comes in higher than forecast, or supply that arrives later or smaller than planned. Stock inaccuracy is another form of supply variation. When the recorded figure is higher than what is physically there, the effect is the same as a delivery arriving short.

Some businesses respond to repeated surprise shortages by raising safety stock. That can hide the problem for a while, but it means carrying extra stock year-round to cover errors that could be fixed at the source. It also makes the buffer less dependable, because part of it may exist only on paper.

Safety stock works best when it covers genuine, unavoidable variation in demand and supply. Improving stock accuracy reduces one avoidable source of variation, so the buffer can be sized for the variation that actually needs covering.

Questions to ask when the plan looks wrong

When a planned order seems unusually late, early or large, it is tempting to begin with forecasts, lead times or planning parameters. Those may be valid causes, but stock accuracy should be checked early in the investigation.

Ask:

  • Does the physical stock match the system quantity for this item at this location?
  • Are there open movements, unprocessed receipts, returns, scrap or transfers that explain the difference?
  • Is the item being handled in the correct unit of measure?
  • Has the item had repeated count variances, and do they point to a process issue?
  • Is the item fast-moving or close enough to needing supply that a small error changes the supply decision?

Starting with these questions can prevent a business from changing planning settings to compensate for what is really a stock-control problem.

These errors also have a direct financial cost. When a count finds less stock than the system shows, the difference is written off. A stock write-off is a straight loss: it reduces profit dollar for dollar, with no sale to offset it. A write-off of $5,000 has the same effect on profit as losing the margin on a much larger amount of sales. Finding and fixing the causes of errors protects the bottom line as well as the plan.

Key Takeaways

  • Stock accuracy means the system on-hand quantity matches the physical quantity for each item and location.
  • A correct inventory total can still hide serious errors on individual items or at individual locations.
  • Time-phased planning carries today's stock error into every future period.
  • Overstated stock delays supply and can cause shortages; understated stock brings supply forward and can create excess.
  • Inaccurate stock leads to unreliable available to promise (ATP) and delivery dates customers can't rely on.
  • Stock errors behave like supply variation; fix them at the source rather than covering them with extra safety stock.
  • Stock write-offs go straight to the bottom line, so preventing errors protects profit as well as the plan.
  • Cycle counting is most valuable when it identifies and removes the causes of recurring errors.
  • Give particular attention to fast-moving items and items where supply is needed soon, where a small discrepancy can change the plan.
